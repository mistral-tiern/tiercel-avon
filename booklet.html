Mistral Tiern: A Death on the Beach

A 5e-Compatible Investigation Module

DM Eyes Only. This document contains the full module: the true solution, investigation scenes, NPCs, shops, and encounters. If you’re a player, stop reading — spoilers ahead.

How to Use This Booklet

This is a murder mystery run as an open investigation, not a linear dungeon crawl. The party can approach leads in almost any order, so this booklet is organized around locations and NPCs rather than a fixed sequence — read The Truth first so you always know what’s really going on underneath whatever the party happens to find first.

Party size 3–6, levels 3–5
Session length Two 3–4 hour sittings (a beach, a bridge, and a manor house’s worth of investigation, plus a final confrontation)
Tone Noble-court intrigue. A staged suicide, an embezzlement scheme, a cover-up that keeps escalating as the killer cleans up loose ends.

Design Note. This module shares a broader world with The Undying Court (a separate one-shot) — different region and era, connected only by light references. See Appendix D: Undying Court Connections at the end of this booklet if you want to use that module’s fixer NPC here, or vice versa. Nothing below assumes you’ve read it.

Setting Overview: The City-State of Mistral Tiern

Mistral Tiern is divided into sixty-four districts, each overseen by a titled noble — Baronets for small or poor regions, up through Dukes for large or strategically important ones. Noble houses are responsible for their own territory; the royal house directly controls four neutral regions not claimed by any house, including the coastline. Temples answer only to their own hierarchy and the Crown — no noble house can supersede a temple’s authority within its walls.

Titles must be granted by the Crown, and every title comes with real financial obligations — land and businesses that must turn a profit or drain the holder dry. Lower titles (Baronet, Baron) have a reputation for corruption and high taxes precisely because they’re the hardest to make profitable. Holders can climb the ladder by purchasing parcels from bankrupt peers, through bribery, through sanctioned dueling, or through Crown-recognized service. This dueling custom matters for the mystery below — it’s the mechanism the killer is trying to exploit.

Banking & Records

The Temple of Numismat functions as Mistral Tiern’s bank — warrior monks who hold coin on account, issue promissory notes, and manage transfers between cities. The Public Records office (accessible via talisman) holds addresses, titles, and employment history for residents — the party’s starting informant provides one.

The Hook

Read or paraphrase:

“Caro Tercelette was found dead on the beach by the Royal Watch. The Watch has ruled the death a suicide. If you investigate this death as a murder and find the person or people responsible, you will receive a payment of 20 gold each.”

The informant’s letter includes Caro’s home address, the name of her bakery (Knead the Dough), and a talisman granting access to the city-state’s public records.

Two equally good framings for who hires the party:

  • A member of the Watch, anonymously, who doesn’t believe the suicide ruling.
  • Zara Tercelette, Caro’s sister and a temple acolyte, who hires the party directly because she doesn’t believe her sister killed herself.

Insight DC 12+ (on the informant, if met in person): this person carries themselves with more authority than their disguise suggests — a Knight, a Guard, or a temple official. Expensive boots, well broken in.

The Truth (Full Solution — DM Only)

Read this before running anything. Nothing below is meant to be dumped on the players wholesale — it’s the ground truth the investigation scenes are built to reveal in pieces.

  1. Caro Tercelette, a blue tiefling, was formerly second-in-command of an “Organization” run out of House Tiercel Avon by Dame Prim, her Baronetess and close friend. The Organization is a profitable illegal side operation — black-market sales of restricted or stolen goods, run under cover of the legitimate holding. Caro left that life to become a baker at Knead the Dough, partnered with innkeeper Marlin Bolger.
  2. Separately, Dame Prim has been embezzling from her own house, helped by her accountant, Ignatius — who uses his lover Esryn’s name and signature on the paperwork without Esryn’s understanding of what he’s signing. This embezzlement plot is unconnected to the murder until the party’s investigation accidentally exposes both at once.
  3. Sir Seccor, Prim’s new second-in-command, wants war and personal glory. He resents Caro — a former second who “abandoned” the Organization and yet still has Prim’s loyalty and affection.
  4. Caro had secretly become a Royal Watch informant, feeding Rhosyn Eida information on Prim’s household. Marlin, protective of Caro, convinced her to break up with her lover, Amara, who remained inside Seccor’s orbit.
  5. Seccor seduces Amara after the breakup and uses her to lure Caro to a private meeting. Three of Seccor’s people — Terra, Cat, and Quinn — kill Caro and stage it as a suicide on the beach.
  6. Once Caro is dead, Seccor starts cleaning up loose ends: Amara is beaten to death and dumped under Crossroads Bridge, on neutral royal territory between House Tiercel Avon and House Goshawk. A bracelet snagged on her clothing (bought by Quinn, per the jeweler Orn) is the clue trail that leads back into Prim’s household.
  7. Seccor then kills Quinn too — another loose end who knows too much — and has the body dumped at Sir Pathos’s manor in House Goshawk. This is deliberate: a murder discovered on the border between two holdings is grounds for the Crown to sanction a duel or war between the two houses. That’s exactly what Seccor wants.
  8. Dame Prim did not order Caro’s murder and does not know about it until the party (or events) inform her — her reaction is genuine shock, not performance. She is guilty of the embezzlement, and of protecting her people reflexively once she suspects something’s wrong.
  9. Ignatius, arrested for the embezzlement, tries to bargain for a lighter sentence by naming names. His first claims — that Dame Prim personally ordered Caro’s murder — are lies, told to save himself by pointing at the most convenient target. Pressed further, he shifts to the truth: Seccor wanted the war, cut Ignatius out of the profits, and it was Seccor — not Prim — behind both killings.

The two crimes, kept straight: Prim is guilty of embezzlement (with Ignatius). Seccor is guilty of murder (Caro, Amara, Quinn) and of trying to manufacture a war by framing Sir Pathos. These threads can resolve independently — a party that exposes one without the other has done real, valid work, and you should let both endings land on their own if that’s how the table gets there.

Design Note. If your table wants a tidier ending, both threads collide naturally at House of Tiercel Avon (see Final Confrontation, below): the party arrives with evidence of both crimes at once, and Prim has to reckon with what her own second-in-command did in her name.

Serpent’s Stein (Aryte, bartender)

A dim tavern with terrariums of snakes. Two drunk patrons (Ronald Montagu, Gavin Fenwick) may interrupt conversation by wrestling near the hearth. Barmaid Wixlee is also present. Low-yield scene — mostly color and a place to overhear rumors.

Temple of Numismat

Warrior monks who run the city’s banking. Confirms Zara’s employment as an acolyte here (also discoverable via Public Records, DC 14+, below). Learning she was sent home after news of Caro’s “suicide” is free information.

Running this scene. Let the Insight checks do real work here — a party that catches the lies at 12+/14+ and keeps pushing gets the true confession at 16+/18+. A party that stops at the first answer walks away believing Prim ordered the murders, which is a legitimate (if wrong) place for the story to go if that’s where your table lands.

Encounters

Street Toughs

Three bandits mistake the party for wandering tourists.

Crack (Thug), x3 — AC 11, HP 50, STR 15 (+2). Multiattack: two melee. Mace +4 to hit, 5 (1d6+2) bludgeoning. Heavy Crossbow +2 to hit, 5 (1d10) piercing. Pack Tactics. CR 1/2 (100 XP each).

Loot: 12 sp total, a mace, a heavy crossbow, a scimitar.

Underdark (En Route to Goblin’s Armpit)

A gargoyle statue blocks a narrow passage (Strength DC 15+ to move it). Beyond: 2x Myconid Sprout, 1x Quaggoth Spore Servant, 1x Grick. Use SRD stat blocks as-is.

Treasure (hidden chest): 1,120 sp, 110 gp, 5 art pieces (25 gp each), 2x Potion of Healing, a Driftglobe, and a Spell Scroll of Find Steed.

Pick the Thief

A DC 12+ Perception check (14+ to catch the act) spots a pickpocket working the crowd. Same stat block as Crack, above, but Dagger (+4 to hit, 1d4+2 piercing) instead of mace/crossbow. Rhosyn intervenes if the party doesn’t (“Nice to see you again, Pick”) — a light beat, not required combat.

Final Confrontation: House of Tiercel Avon

Returning here with accusations turns hostile. Dame Prim and Sir Seccor fight alongside their knights if the party pushes the confrontation to violence. They’re built from two different SRD blocks, lightly reskinned, so the fight reads as two different people rather than a mirror match.

Sir Seccor — reskinned SRD Knight. The aggressor, fighting because he started this and has nothing left to lose.

Multiattack. Two melee attacks, or two ranged attacks. Rapier (Duelist’s Blade). +5 to hit, 7 (1d8+3) piercing — treat as a shortsword mechanically. Heavy Crossbow. +3 to hit, range 100/400 ft., 6 (1d10+1) piercing.

Scaling Note. Both blocks above are full SRD-strength (CR 3 each) — a real Deadly fight for a 3-player, level 3 party if both are pressed to their full stat blocks at once. For a softer confrontation (or a lower-level table), drop Seccor’s AC to 11 (leather instead of plate) and Prim’s to 13 (studded leather), or simply have Prim’s knights hold back while she attempts to talk first — she has more reason to want this resolved without bloodshed than Seccor does.

Treasure: Level up. 9 gems (100 gp each), 400 cp, 6,000 sp, 1,300 gp, 170 pp. The Crown offers the party knighthoods if they take up the Tiercel Avon holdings — no one else currently wants a Baronet title this troubled.

Multiple Endings. This doesn’t have to end in combat. A party that’s built a strong enough case (the Ignatius confession, the bracelet, the staged blood evidence at Goshawk) can bring this to the Crown as an accusation instead — Seccor stands trial or duels for his crimes, Prim faces separate consequences for the embezzlement, and the “final confrontation” becomes a tense negotiation instead of a fight. Both are valid endings; let the party’s evidence decide which one you run.

Lore Note: Lycanthropy in Mistral Tiern. The curse can be deliberately taken on — wear a wolfskin cloak and belt for twenty-eight days and nights starting on a full moon, then drink a special beer brewed from water collected from wolves’ pawprints, on the next full moon. It also runs in bloodlines: a family’s seventh-born child is always born witch or werewolf, and House Talon (which keeps ravens) names itself godparent to every seventh son born within its holdings. This isn’t incidental — House Talon’s own head, Marquis Balen Wulfram, is himself a werewolf, and his mother and former regent, Lady Lendri Thorn, is a Lythari (an elven lycanthropic shapeshifter) originally from the Feywild. Werewolf bloodlines in this setting trace back to the Harz Mountains north of the city, where the first werewolves were created as soldiers in service to witches — a history that eventually turned into open war between witches, werewolves, and a vampire clutch, which the werewolves won. Useful color for Caleb Brocken, above (note the shared surname with House Talon’s current footman, Sandulf Brocken — not a coincidence). See the separate Hexenderwulfes sequel notes (unfinished draft) for the full story this lore was building toward.

Lore Note: The Out of Time Cafe’s Barista. She’s gone by Gwyneira Gentry, Eiragwyn, and Siarad Serenlaw (“Enna” to those who know her better) at different points — treat this as deliberate, not a continuity error. Eladrin don’t give out their true names, and a fey running a shop literally called Out of Time, with a portal mirror as its centerpiece, is exactly the kind of person who’d wear a different name for different customers or different days. A DM can play this straight (she genuinely introduces herself differently each visit) or reveal it as a plot point later (someone who’s spoken to her three times under three names starts to notice).

Side Quests (Optional)

These aren’t connected to the murder — pull them out if you want breathing room between investigation beats, or cut them entirely without losing anything.

  • Goldbringer’s Daughter. Dawali Goldbringer, a dwarf searching for his missing daughter, offers 50 gp for help — but the map he’s using (a cursed Magical Map of Ineptitude) is worthless and actively misleading. Play for comedy or tragedy as your table prefers.
  • Temple of Deneir. Rose Blackburn wants a stolen family locket recovered from Cloud Shadow, a black tabaxi rogue with one white foot.
  • Temple of Savras. Rizzacrae asks the party to deliver a sealed bronze box (do not open it) to a goblin contact, Kikkit, in the mountains near Tuite Thorp.

Scaling Notes

Situation Suggested Adjustment
Small party (3), low level (3) Run as written. The final confrontation and the Underdark encounter are the difficulty spikes to watch.
Large party (5–6), any level Add a second knight to the final confrontation (reuse Rhosyn’s stat block, reskinned) to keep the action economy honest.
Any size, higher level (4–5) Raise interview/investigation DCs by 1–2 across the board — the clue structure holds up fine, it’s the numbers that go stale. Let Ignatius’s jail scene stay at listed DCs; it’s meant to be gettable.
Table wants more combat The Underdark route to Goblin’s Armpit can be expanded with additional short encounters; the Street Toughs and Pick the Thief scenes can both be run as forced encounters rather than avoidable ones.
Table wants less combat The final confrontation converts cleanly to a negotiation/trial scene (see Multiple Endings, above) if the party builds a strong enough case beforehand.

Prim embezzles, with Ignatius (using Esryn’s signature) → Seccor wants war and resents Caro → Caro is a Watch informant, breaks up with Amara → Seccor seduces Amara, uses her to lure Caro → Terra, Cat, Quinn kill Caro, staged as suicide → Seccor kills Amara (loose end; bracelet clue points to Quinn) → Seccor kills Quinn (loose end), dumps body at Goshawk to frame Sir Pathos and provoke a war → party investigation exposes the embezzlement (Ignatius/Prim) and, separately, the murders (Seccor) → Ignatius, arrested, lies about Prim first, then gives up Seccor when pushed.

Appendix B: Suggested Fixed Timeline (Optional)

The investigation is written to unfold whenever the party triggers each discovery — that’s the default, and it works well for a party that pokes around and follows leads at its own pace. If your table wants a firmer structure (or you want NPCs to react in real time whether or not the party’s paying attention), use this fixed timeline instead. Nothing here changes the clues themselves — only when each body is found and each courier arrives.

Day Events
Day 1 Hook delivered at dawn. Beach crime scene. Victim’s Street, Victim’s Home, interview with Zara. Half-Way Inn / Knead the Dough / Serpent’s Stein. (Optional: Street Toughs encounter en route to the victim’s home, if you want the talisman stolen and Rhosyn drawn in early.)
Day 2 Courier delivers news of Amara’s body at Crossroads Bridge. Investigate the scene, interview Rhosyn. Follow the bracelet clue to Half-Way Inn (if not already visited) and to Dame Prim. Public Records history checks. Tipsy Tankard — interview Beko, search upstairs.
Day 3 Courier delivers news of Quinn’s body at House of Goshawk. Investigate the scene, interview Sir Pathos.
Day 4 Underdark journey to Goblin’s Armpit (gargoyle door, fungal creatures, treasure). Interview Balor. First visit to the Manor of Ignatius and Esryn.
Day 5 Return visit to Esryn; search the manor; Ignatius reported and arrested. Royal Tower Jail interview with Ignatius the same afternoon.
Day 6 Final Confrontation at House of Tiercel Avon (or the negotiated/trial ending, if the party’s case is strong enough).

Running it this way. Each courier arrives whether or not the party has finished the previous day’s leads — that’s the point of a fixed timeline, and it can create real pressure if the party is still working through Day 1 when the Day 2 courier shows up. If that pressure doesn’t suit your table, treat the days above as a soft suggestion and let discoveries slip a day rather than stacking two couriers on top of each other.

Appendix C: Example Playthrough

A trimmed record of one table’s route through this material — useful for pacing reference, not a script to follow. Names below (Kaun, Vinny, Ross) are that table’s specific characters.

After finding the bakery closed, the party checked the Serpent’s Stein next door, talking to bartender Aryte amid two drunk regulars wrestling by the hearth. From there, Half-Way Inn: Marlin confirmed his business partnership with Caro and directed them toward Zara at the Temple of Numismat, where they learned she’d been sent home after news of the “suicide.” En route to the sisters’ home, the party was mugged — one character killed, the Public Records talisman stolen. Rhosyn Eida arrived, assigned a local knight (Ross) to investigate the mugging, and that knight ended up unofficially helping with the Caro case too, on the condition the stolen talisman was never reported.

Ross and the survivor searched Caro and Zara’s home, finding the empty savings jar, the poison vials, the hidden journal, and the missing items from Zara’s room. They tracked Amara to the Tipsy Tankard, where Beko redirected them to the Goblin’s Armpit in the Underdark, with directions through the tunnels.

The next morning, a second letter arrived: a body under Crossroads Bridge. It was Amara, beaten to death — Rhosyn confirmed the connection and paid the party 20 gold for their progress, while officially maintaining the suicide ruling.

The party then went to Half-Way Inn again and learned about the bracelet (tying to Quinn), checked Tipsy Tankard upstairs (finding Zara’s stolen bag and bloodstained earrings), and were interrupted by a third letter: Quinn’s body, found at House of Goshawk. The staging (too little blood for the wound) tipped them off that Quinn was killed elsewhere.

A detour into the Underdark for Goblin’s Armpit produced a side encounter (gargoyle door, fungal creatures, a treasure chest) before reaching Balor, who confirmed Amara’s absence from work and pointed toward Seccor. The party then pursued the Ignatius/Esryn embezzlement thread as a separate lead — searching the manor, finding the accounting book and Numismat talisman, and helping Esryn report Ignatius to the Watch. Once jailed, Ignatius offered his bargain: blaming Prim first (caught as a lie on Insight), then giving up the true confession implicating Seccor.

Appendix E: Feywild Side Quest — The Night Dancers (Optional, IN PROGRESS — resolution not yet finalized, see below)

A Twelve Dancing Princesses riff — reframed here with nine Eladrin princesses and a nasty twist. Fully standalone; nothing below connects to the murder investigation. Entry point is the Out of Time Cafe (see NPCs & Shops), already in the main location list.

What’s organized vs. what’s still yours to decide. The material below is faithfully structured from your notes — hook, encounters, NPC roster, rewards. What’s not here is a stated resolution: what the party needs to actually prove, and what happens once they do. See the callout at the end for the open questions before you run this.

The Hook

The Out of Time Cafe, run by a barista who goes by Gwyneira Gentry (or Eiragwyn, or Siarad — she never gives the same name twice; see the Lore Note under NPCs & Shops) — an Eladrin with delicate stag horns woven with flowering vines — is full of mismatched mechanical timepieces, none showing the same time. A full-length antique gilded mirror serves as the shop’s centerpiece — and a portal.

Read or paraphrase:

You stumble through the gilded antique mirror into a sun-dappled meadow, bordered on one side by thick dark woods and on the other by hundred-foot-tall hedges. An elaborate dais rises at the meadow’s center. A well-dressed Summer Eladrin prince, crowned in gold and surrounded by elven knights, presides over a crowd of fey — gnomes, satyrs, pixies, centaurs.

A Winter Eladrin knight kneels before him, face stoic, as the prince announces: “This knight has failed in his quest and must suffer the agreed-upon price. He is to be imprisoned with the others until someone solves the mystery of my daughters’ slippers.”

Behind you, the mirror is gone. No portal, no way back. The only paths out of the meadow are through the frightening dark forest, or through the single entrance to a hundred-foot hedge maze. The crowd falls silent, then follows the condemned knight into the maze, toward its center. You have nowhere else to go but after them.

The party is trapped until they solve the mystery themselves — the failed knight isn’t the first to be imprisoned this way (“the others”).

Cervine Fey — A once-worshipped fae spirit, shapeshifts between a red or white deer and a woman with a deer’s face, eyes like frosted glass, straw-yellow hair. Herds local deer as “cattle,” choosing which are taken by hunters. Encountered at a liminal threshold (flickering lights, blue twilight) — heading toward an unseen ocean whose waves can be heard but not placed. A distant white light in the fog invites the party to follow; doing so fills them with dread rather than reward. Best used as an atmospheric wandering encounter, not a fight.
